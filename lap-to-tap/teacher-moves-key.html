Teacher moves key

36planned moves in the model, in the order they happen, with the reason for each. Move names match the Teacher Response Scripts.

Before class

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
1Strategic seatingTier 2/3 students on the center aisle, reteach students in rows 1–2, quick finishers spread along the walls.The teacher reaches the four highest-need students in one pass, and no quick finisher sits beside an audience.
2Posted lap marks✓ ★ ○ ● anchor chart at the front of the room.Feedback happens without talking. Students know what a mark means and what to do next.

Launch

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
3What to Do direction“Pencil on number one. Plot point A.”Most stalls start with an unclear first step. Naming the first physical action removes it.
4Delay and return (Lightning 1)“Is this for a grade?” “Good question. After your first point. Pencil on number one.” She keeps walking.The question is real but the timing is not. One line, no audience, straight back to the task.

Compliance lap

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
5ProximityDown the center aisle first: T1, T2, T3, T4.Every Tier 2/3 student sees the teacher in the first 30 seconds, before behavior starts.
6Silent recognition✓ in the margin of on-task papers.On-task work gets noticed without stopping anyone.
7Private check-in (sleeper)Crouches, quiet voice: “Sit up with me. What do you need to get going?”Sleeping is a barrier to the student’s own learning. A public correction adds an audience to a student who is already shut down.
8Planned return visitsSame calm visit the second and third time. No escalation.Consistency without a power struggle. Head down is never a Tap Out on its own.
9Precise praise with a point“Kai, already on point B. Responsible point.” Snaps follow.Pays the expectation and keeps praise ahead of correction, four to one.

Mastery lap

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
10Data-first routeStarts in rows 1–2 with the reteach students.Misconceptions show up first where students are furthest behind.
11Academic marks★ on correct answers, ○ around a spot to look again, ● where nothing is there yet.Precise feedback in one stroke. The student knows exactly where to look.
12Diagnose the need (refuser)Privately: “What’s getting in the way?” The student: “I just don’t understand.”Refusal is often not understanding. Ask before assuming defiance.
13Reduce the task (dot strategy)“Let’s put a dot at that one. Go on to the next. If you don’t know that one, put a dot, go on to the next, and after I’m done with my checks, I’ll come back around.”The student keeps working and gets an entry point. No stand-off over one problem.
14Promised returnShe comes back to the refuser and starts at the dots.Trust. The dot tells the teacher exactly where to start.
15Release responsibility★ for a quick finisher, then: “Done early? Check your work, then read or do extended work.”Quick finishers have a next step ready before they create their own.
16Pattern-based pauseThree or more ○ on point A: “I’ve talked to a lot of you and heard the same thing.”One reteach beats twenty individual ones, and naming it as common takes the shame out of the mistake.

Attention getter

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
17Attention signalThe same sound cue every time.Students know what it means without an explanation.
18Strong Voice: scan and waitStands still, silent, looking across the room.She does not talk over students. The wait does the work.
19Positive narration first“Thank you for eyes, [name]. One. Thank you, everybody.”Directs attention to the students meeting the expectation before addressing anyone who is not.
20Economy of language warning“[Name], warning, voices are off.” Said once.Names the step on the ladder. Short means there is nothing to argue with.
21Anonymous group correction“For anyone continuing, I’m marking. I’ll lap around and let you know if you were one of the ones I identified.”Addresses everyone without naming anyone. The individual follow-up happens later, privately.
22Planned ignoringT1 mutters “whatever.” She tallies and keeps going.The expectation was already said. Answering the last word feeds the audience.

Misconception example

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
23Proximity while teachingWalks to T1’s desk (pencil tapping) and keeps teaching from there.Corrects without stopping the lesson or saying the student’s name.
24Non-verbal cue (Lightning 2)Hand-up signal and keeps teaching. Calls on the student once the hand is up, then answers for everyone.Honors the question, reteaches the routine, and the lesson never stops.
25Check for understandingFinger check: “(2, 5) and (5, 2). Same point or different?”Every student answers at once. She sees who still has it wrong.

The ladder

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
26Seat change“Bring your paper to Reset 1.” Calm, private, to a seat chosen in advance.Changes the audience. A pre-set seat leaves nothing to negotiate.
27Written reflectionPlaces the form on the desk. No lecture.The student stays in the room and moves from performing to thinking.
28Request a Tap In early“I need Tap In support with at least one student.”Asks for support at Level 2, before a removal, without naming or spotlighting the student.
29Disengage and protect the lessonTallies and keeps teaching while support is on the way.The majority’s learning comes first. Re-engaging would hand the student a stage.
30Spectator notice“If you’re choosing to engage with it, as a spectator or a participant, that is where your focus is.”Names the choice for everyone. The audience becomes responsible for its own focus.
31Precise positive narration“Thank you to my three students whose eyes are up. Focused, ready to hear this next part.” Points and snaps.Moves the room’s attention and the reward to the students doing it right.
32Document while teachingMarks on the seating chart and lap tracker.The record of every step travels with the student if a Tap Out follows.

Hallway

#MoveWhat it looked and sounded likeWhy that one, then
33Body positioningStudents side by side, facing away from the door window, with the teacher between them and the class.No audience and a safe sightline.
34Higher expectation, no label“You miss an opportunity to shine. You miss an opportunity to turn it around. Yeah, you fell short, but then you bounce back. So you choose.”The expectation stays high, the choice stays with the student, and the student’s dignity stays intact.
35Separate the spectatorsTheir own line. They go back in first; the primary student goes back last.No one walks back into an audience.
36Document with support in placeVoice form while the Tap In responder holds the class.The record gets made without leaving the class unsupervised.

Other moves that fit the same moments

From the Teacher Response Scripts, least invasive first. Any of these is a sound choice in the same moment.

Call-outsNon-verbal hand-up signal and keep teaching · Economy of language: “Hand.” · Delay and return: “I want your answer. I need the hand first.”
Talking after a warningNon-verbal cue: stand near and tap the page · Private individual correction: “Voice off, eyes on number four. Thank you.” · Time-bound choice: “Two more minutes silent, then you can check with your partner.”
Arguing or talking backDefer the conversation: “I hear you. Come see me at the end of the block.” · Limit, then silence: “I am not going to go back and forth in front of everybody.”
Head down or asleepOffer a reset: “Get some water and come right back.” · Movement: “Stand at the back counter and work there for five minutes.” · Reduce the task: “Just the first problem. I will check back.”
Not startingCheck for understanding: “Tell me what number one is asking.” · Choice of entry: “You can do this one with me, or with a partner. Your call.”
